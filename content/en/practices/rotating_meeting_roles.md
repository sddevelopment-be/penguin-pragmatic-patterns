## Problem statement

People tend to fall into the same roles in meetings, so the same voices drive agendas, take notes, and frame decisions. Everyone else sits back, disengages, or leaves with little sense of ownership.

## Intent

* Spread facilitation, documenting, and decision-challenging duties across the group.
* Expose more teammates to the mechanics of effective meetings.
* Make it normal to question assumptions and surface blind spots.

## Solution

1. **List essential roles.** Typical rotations include facilitator, timekeeper, scribe, and devil’s advocate or risk spotter.
2. **Publish a roster.** Map the next few meetings and assign different teammates to each role, allowing swaps when conflicts arise.
3. **Provide guardrails.** Share agendas, note templates, or checklists so new role owners know what “good” looks like.
4. **Check in quickly.** Spend a few minutes after each meeting to capture feedback from role owners and update the roster.
5. **Store outputs centrally.** Post notes, decisions, and follow-ups in a shared location so continuity does not depend on an individual.

Keep the rotation lightweight. When a meeting is trivial, shrink the number of roles; when it’s high-stakes, pair newcomers with experienced hosts.

## Contextual forces

### Enablers
The following factors support effective application of the practice:

* The team values facilitation craftsmanship and is willing to invest prep time.
* Psychological safety makes it acceptable to practise in front of peers.
* Meetings have repeatable cadence, giving everyone a chance to take a turn.

### Deterrents
The following factors prevent effective application of the practice:

* Leadership insists on controlling every agenda or update.
* Individuals do not have capacity to prepare or follow through on assigned roles.
* Meetings are ad hoc fire-fights where deliberate facilitation feels unrealistic.

## Rationale

Rotating roles distributes power and expertise. When everyone practises facilitation, challenge, and documentation, meetings stop depending on a single organiser. That redundancy is critical when people are absent and makes continuous improvement part of the cadence.

## Application

### Consequences

* **Learning curve:** Early rotations can feel awkward or artificially scripted.
* **Variable quality:** Notes or decisions may be inconsistent while people gain experience.
* **Role fatigue:** Without tracking, the same conscientious teammates may shoulder the work.

### Mitigation strategies

* **Rotate gradually:** Start with note-taking before handing off facilitation.
* **Pair newcomers:** Let an experienced host shadow or co-facilitate the first few times.
* **Review the roster:** Use retrospectives to rebalance assignments and celebrate improvements.

## Examples

* **Weekly sync:** Each week a different engineer facilitates, another records actions in the shared document, and a third calls timeboxes to keep the agenda on track.
* **Design review:** Product, engineering, and design alternate leading the conversation while a rotating devil’s advocate tests assumptions and highlights risks before decisions are made.

## Criticism & Clarifications

- Not every meeting warrants the full set of roles; keep the structure proportional to the stakes.
- High-risk sessions may still require a dedicated facilitator—rotate roles where the learning payoff is worth it.
- Make opt-outs acceptable when cognitive load, accessibility needs, or power dynamics would make a rotation harmful.
