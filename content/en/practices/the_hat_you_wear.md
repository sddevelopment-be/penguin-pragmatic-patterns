## Problem statement

When people swap between different responsibilities mid-conversation—mentor, reviewer, decision-maker, peer—their teammates have to guess which voice is speaking. That uncertainty slows decisions, dilutes feedback, and causes emotional whiplash.

## Intent

* Make the stance you are taking explicit before you speak.
* Help others respond appropriately—debate, absorb, escalate, or act.
* Prevent meetings from drifting because roles and expectations are unclear.

## Solution

1. **Declare the hat.** Before you offer input, state the perspective you are taking: “I’m wearing my reviewer hat,” “This is a product decision,” or “Let me switch into coaching mode.”
2. **Describe the expectation.** Briefly articulate what you need back: alignment, challenge, questions, or simply acknowledgement.
3. **Invite confirmations or swaps.** Encourage others to claim their hats too—maybe someone else should take the decision or facilitate.
4. **Reset deliberately.** When the conversation moves into a different mode, explicitly call out the switch so everyone tracks the change.

Keep the phrasing lightweight and adapt it to team culture; the goal is clarity, not ceremony.

## Contextual forces

### Enablers
The following factors support effective application of the practice:

* Meetings regularly mix decision-making, brainstorming, and coaching.
* Team members have multiple responsibilities (e.g. tech lead, IC, mentor) and swap frequently.
* Psychological safety allows people to request clarity without fear.

### Deterrents
The following factors prevent effective application of the practice:

* Hierarchical environments where speaking up about roles feels risky.
* Fast-moving incident response where signalling is skipped under pressure.
* Teams that already struggle with conflict may interpret hat declarations as controlling.

## Rationale

Explicit role signalling reduces ambiguity. Instead of burning energy on “What are they expecting from me?”, you can focus on the work. It also creates a shared language for switching between mentoring, deciding, and exploring—making context shifts intentional instead of accidental.

## Application

### Consequences

* **Awkward at first:** Calling out hats can feel contrived until the habit sticks.
* **Role policing:** Overzealous use may sound bureaucratic or shut down organic conversation.
* **Missed nuance:** Some contributions blend roles; forcing a single hat can flatten useful complexity.

### Mitigation strategies

* **Model the behaviour:** Leaders and facilitators should go first and keep the tone casual.
* **Use prompts:** Add hats to agenda items or meeting templates so everyone remembers to declare them.
* **Allow dual hats:** It is fine to say “I’m part reviewer, part coach here” when the distinction matters.

## Examples

* **Code review:** “Switching to reviewer mode: the algorithm is elegant, but the error handling needs another pass.”
* **Planning meeting:** “Product hat on—I need a commit here. Can we confirm scope or push the release?”
* **1:1 coaching:** “Wearing my mentor hat; I’m not making a decision, just offering ideas you can accept or discard.”

## Criticism & Clarifications

- The practice clarifies intent, but it does not grant authority—teams should still agree on who decides what.
- Avoid using hats as weapons (“I’m the boss, so...”)—pair the declaration with respectful dialogue.
- In crisis mode, use lightweight cues (“Decision now”) rather than lengthy explanations.
